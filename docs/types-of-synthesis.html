Types of Music Synthesis

How do synthesisers create sound? Here are six approaches, how they work, what they sound like, and some instruments that use them.

↓ Download printable worksheet (PDF)

What is synthesis?

Sound synthesis means creating or shaping sound using technology. A synthesiser might generate waveforms electronically, combine oscillators, manipulate tiny pieces of recorded sound, or use mathematical models of vibrating objects. Many instruments combine several approaches.

Six ways to create sound

01 · Filtering sound

Subtractive Synthesis

Start with a harmonically rich waveform, such as a sawtooth or square wave, and use a filter to remove some of its frequencies. Changing the filter cutoff makes the sound brighter or darker; envelopes can make the sound change over time.

Typical sounds: Bass lines, leads, pads and classic synthesiser tones.

Examples: Moog Minimoog, Roland SH-101, Korg MS-20.

02 · Electronic circuits

Analog Synthesis

Analog synthesis uses electronic circuits to generate and shape continuously varying electrical signals. Oscillators commonly produce sine, triangle, sawtooth and square waves, which can then be shaped by filters and amplifiers.

Typical sounds: Warm pads, basses, leads and evolving textures.

Examples: Moog Minimoog, Roland Jupiter-8, ARP 2600.

Note: Analog describes how the sound is produced; subtractive describes a way of shaping it. An analog synth can also be subtractive.

03 · Oscillators interacting

FM Synthesis

Frequency modulation (FM) uses one oscillator, called the modulator, to change the frequency of another, called the carrier. This produces additional frequencies and can create complex, bright or metallic tones.

Typical sounds: Bells, electric pianos, metallic percussion and evolving timbres.

Examples: Yamaha DX7, Yamaha TX81Z, Elektron Digitone II.

04 · Changing wave shapes

Wavetable Synthesis

A wavetable contains a collection of different waveforms. The synthesiser plays a waveform from the table and can move or blend between positions, changing the sound's harmonic character over time.

Typical sounds: Moving pads, digital textures and expressive, evolving tones.

Examples: PPG Wave, Waldorf Microwave, Korg Modwave.

05 · Tiny fragments of sound

Granular Synthesis

Granular synthesis divides a sound into very short fragments called grains. These grains can be repeated, overlapped, reordered, stretched or played at different pitches to create entirely new textures.

Typical sounds: Atmospheres, soundscapes, shimmering clouds and unusual textures.

Examples: Tasty Chips GR Mega, Granulator II, Quanta.

06 · Simulating instruments

Physical Modelling

Physical modelling uses mathematics to simulate how a physical object vibrates and produces sound—for example, a plucked string, a struck bar or the air column inside a wind instrument. It models the behaviour of the instrument rather than playing a recording of it.

Typical sounds: Plucked strings, piano-like tones, brass, woodwinds and other acoustic instrument sounds.

Examples: Yamaha VL1, AAS Chromaphone, Pianoteq.
