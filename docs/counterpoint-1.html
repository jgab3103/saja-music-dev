Counterpoint: the art of independent voices

Counterpoint is the art of combining distinct melodic lines so that each retains its own identity while contributing to a coherent musical whole. Its history connects medieval song, Renaissance sacred music, Baroque fugue and modern composition.

This introduction explores the ideas behind counterpoint before moving to practical study of the five species associated with Johann Joseph Fux.

01 / THE LONG HISTORY

From polyphony to counterpoint

Counterpoint was not invented by a single composer or theorist. It emerged over centuries as musicians explored how several voices could move together without losing their independence.

Early European polyphony grew from practices of adding voices to chant. By the later Middle Ages, composers were writing increasingly elaborate combinations of melodic lines. Renaissance composers developed a sophisticated art of vocal polyphony in which imitation, carefully controlled dissonance and the shaping of individual lines became central.

9TH–13TH CENTURIES

Early polyphony and organum

Written sources document experiments with multiple voices, from relatively simple parallel motion to the elaborate organum associated with Notre-Dame composers such as Léonin and Pérotin.

14TH–15TH CENTURIES

Increasing rhythmic and melodic independence

Late medieval and early Renaissance music develops intricate rhythmic relationships and imitative techniques. Composers such as Guillaume de Machaut and Johannes Ockeghem reveal different possibilities for combining voices.

16TH CENTURY

Renaissance imitative polyphony

Josquin des Prez, Giovanni Pierluigi da Palestrina and others develop fluid vocal textures in which melodic lines enter, answer and overlap. This tradition becomes a major reference point for later teaching of strict counterpoint.

17TH–18TH CENTURIES

Counterpoint in a tonal world

As tonal harmony becomes increasingly influential, composers combine linear independence with harmonic direction. The fugue becomes an especially important vehicle for contrapuntal invention.

19TH–20TH CENTURIES

Counterpoint as craft, study and reinvention

Composers and theorists revisit earlier styles while adapting contrapuntal thinking to chromatic harmony, new tonal languages and eventually atonal music.

02 / JOHANN JOSEPH FUX

Fux and Gradus ad Parnassum

Johann Joseph Fux (1660–1741) was an Austrian composer and theorist. His Gradus ad Parnassum (1725), written in Latin, became one of the most influential counterpoint textbooks in Western music education.

Presented as a dialogue between teacher and student, the treatise teaches contrapuntal writing in carefully ordered stages. Its model is an idealised form of the older vocal style often associated with Palestrina. It does not describe every feature of Renaissance composition, nor does it provide a complete account of Bach's tonal practice.

The enduring pedagogical idea is simple: learn to control a small number of musical relationships before attempting more complex textures. Students begin with a fixed melody, the cantus firmus, and compose another voice against it.

A useful distinction: “Fuxian counterpoint” is a teaching method based on a stylised version of earlier polyphony. It is not the only kind of counterpoint, and its rules should not be mistaken for universal laws of composition.
03 / J. S. BACH

Bach: counterpoint as musical architecture

Johann Sebastian Bach (1685–1750) brought contrapuntal techniques to extraordinary levels of complexity and expressive range. His works combine independent melodic lines with a powerful sense of tonal harmony, rhythm and large-scale structure.

The Well-Tempered Clavier contains preludes and fugues in all major and minor keys across two books. The Inventions and Sinfonias offer especially clear examples of two- and three-part writing. In The Art of Fugue, Bach explores the possibilities of transforming and combining a single musical subject.

Bach and Fux were contemporaries. It would be misleading to describe Bach's counterpoint as an evolution from Fux's textbook: the two represent overlapping but distinct strands of the European contrapuntal tradition. Fux codified a pedagogical approach to strict counterpoint; Bach's music offers a rich model of tonal and motivic counterpoint.

04 / CONTRAPUNTAL FORMS & TECHNIQUES

Canon, fugue and imitation

Technique & form

Canon

A melody is imitated by another voice according to a defined rule, often after a time delay. The familiar round is a simple canon. More elaborate canons may use inversion, augmentation or other transformations.

Contrapuntal form

Fugue

A composition built around a recurring subject, typically introduced successively in different voices. Episodes, further entries, stretto and other devices can develop the musical argument. A fugue is not simply a canon: its voices are freer after their entries.

Technique

Imitation

One voice takes up a melodic idea heard in another. The imitation may be exact or varied. It is fundamental to much Renaissance polyphony and Baroque contrapuntal writing.

Technique

Invertible counterpoint

Two or more lines are written so they can exchange their relative positions—for example, an upper voice becoming a lower voice—while preserving acceptable harmonic relationships.

Counterpoint is also a way of thinking rather than a single form. It appears in motets, masses, inventions, chorale preludes, fugues, string quartets and countless other kinds of music.

05 / BOOKS THAT SHAPED THE DISCIPLINE

Important texts on counterpoint

Gioseffo Zarlino — Le istitutioni harmoniche1558

A major Renaissance theoretical work addressing consonance, dissonance, modes and the practice of combining voices.

Johann Joseph Fux — Gradus ad Parnassum1725

The classic systematic presentation of species counterpoint. Its staged exercises influenced generations of composers and teachers.

Luigi Cherubini — Cours de contrepoint et de fugue1835

An influential nineteenth-century treatment of counterpoint and fugue in the conservatoire tradition.

Arnold Schoenberg — Preliminary Exercises in CounterpointPublished posthumously, 1963; edited by Leonard Stein

Schoenberg's teaching approaches contrapuntal discipline through exercises in melodic independence and voice combination. Although Schoenberg is associated with atonality and twelve-tone composition, this book begins with foundational contrapuntal craft.

Kent Kennan — CounterpointFirst published 1959

A widely used modern textbook focused on eighteenth-century tonal counterpoint, complementing rather than duplicating Fux's older-style species approach.

Knud Jeppesen — Counterpoint: The Polyphonic Vocal Style of the Sixteenth CenturyEnglish edition, 1939

A historically oriented study of sixteenth-century vocal polyphony, particularly the style associated with Palestrina.

Two traditions worth distinguishing: species counterpoint trains the handling of melodic intervals and dissonance in a controlled setting; tonal counterpoint adds the harmonic and formal priorities characteristic of much eighteenth-century music.
06 / LEARNING BY COMPOSING

The five species of Fux

Fux's method builds complexity gradually. Each species introduces a new rhythmic relationship to the cantus firmus, and with it new questions about consonance, dissonance and melodic continuity.

I

Note against note

One note in the added voice for every cantus note. Focus on consonance and independence.

II

Two against one

Two notes against each cantus note. Introduces carefully controlled weak-beat dissonance.

III

Four against one

Four notes against each cantus note. Develops melodic motion and passing figures.

IV

Syncopation

Tied notes create suspensions, with attention to preparation and resolution.

V

Florid counterpoint

Combines rhythmic patterns and techniques from the preceding species.
